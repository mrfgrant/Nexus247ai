What Is a Nexus Letter — and Why Does It Matter?

A nexus letter is a medical opinion that connects your current diagnosis to something that happened during your military service. Without it, the VA has no professional basis to link your condition to your time in uniform — and that's where most claims fail.

Under 38 CFR § 3.303, the VA requires three things to grant service connection:

  1. A current, diagnosed medical condition
  2. An in-service event, injury, or illness
  3. A medical nexus linking the two

That third element — the nexus — is what kills most claims. Veterans have the diagnosis. Veterans have the service records. But without a document that explicitly connects them using language the VA recognizes, the rater has no choice but to deny.

⚠️
Why most claims are denied
The VA's own data shows that the majority of initial denials occur because no nexus was established, or the nexus letter submitted was vague, improperly formatted, or lacked the required probability language. This is not a medical problem. It's a documentation problem — and it's fixable.

Can You Write a Nexus Letter Yourself?

Technically, a nexus letter must be signed by a qualified medical professional — an MD, DO, NP, PA, or psychologist. The VA will not accept a letter signed only by the veteran.

However — and this is the part most veterans never hear — you can write the letter yourself and bring it to your provider to review and sign. Most providers are happy to sign a well-researched, well-formatted letter because it saves them hours of work. You're doing the heavy lifting. They're providing their professional opinion.

The Exact Structure of a Winning Nexus Letter

Every effective nexus letter has six components. Miss any one of them and the VA rater will assign it less weight — or disregard it entirely.

Section 01
Provider Credentials & Introduction
Opens with the provider's full name, credentials (MD, NP, PA, etc.), specialty, years of experience, and a statement that they have reviewed your complete medical records. This establishes the letter's credibility before the opinion is given.

Example: "I am [Name], a licensed Nurse Practitioner with 14 years of clinical experience in [specialty]. I have reviewed the complete medical history, service treatment records, and VA records of [Veteran Name]."
Section 02
Current Diagnosis
Clearly states the veteran's current diagnosed condition using the proper clinical terminology. Do not be vague. "The veteran experiences back pain" is weak. "The veteran carries a current diagnosis of Lumbosacral Strain, ICD-10 M54.5" is strong.
Section 03
In-Service Event or Exposure
Describes the specific in-service event, injury, or exposure that is being linked to the current condition. Reference your service records, MOS, deployment history, or buddy statements here. The more specific, the better.
Section 04
Medical Rationale & Literature
This is where most self-written letters fall short. The provider must explain why the connection exists — not just assert it. Cite peer-reviewed medical literature that supports the link between the in-service event and the current condition. The VA assigns significantly more weight to opinions backed by published research.
Section 05
The Magic Phrase — Probability Language
The VA requires a specific probability statement. This is the most important single sentence in the entire letter.

Use exactly this language: "It is at least as likely as not (50% or greater probability) that the veteran's [condition] is related to [in-service event]."

"At least as likely as not" is the legal threshold for service connection under the benefit of the doubt rule (38 CFR § 3.102). Any weaker language — "possibly," "may be related," "could be connected" — gives the VA grounds to deny.
38 CFR § 3.102 — Benefit of the Doubt
"When there is an approximate balance of positive and negative evidence regarding any issue material to the determination of a matter, the Secretary shall give the benefit of the doubt to the claimant." The threshold is "at least as likely as not" — not "certain," not "probable." Meet this standard and the VA must grant service connection.
Section 06
Signature, Credentials & Contact
The letter must be signed on official letterhead (or include the provider's full credentials), with a contact number or email the VA can use to verify. An unsigned or uncredentialed letter carries no weight.

Writing a Secondary Service Connection Letter

Secondary conditions — disabilities caused by or aggravated by an already service-connected condition — follow the same structure but require one additional element: you must establish the causal chain explicitly.

🔗
Secondary connection example — our founder's case
Tinnitus (10%, direct service connection) → Major Depressive Disorder (70%, secondary to Tinnitus) → Hypertension (not rated, secondary to MDD) → Chronic Kidney Disease Stage 3 (30%, secondary to Hypertension). One ringing ear from military service ultimately established kidney disease compensation — because the chain was documented correctly at every link.

For secondary conditions, Section 03 of your letter changes to describe the existing service-connected condition — not the in-service event. The probability language in Section 05 becomes: "It is at least as likely as not that [secondary condition] was caused or aggravated by the veteran's service-connected [primary condition]."

The Most Common Mistakes That Get Letters Rejected

  • Weak probability language — "possibly related" or "may be connected" does not meet the legal threshold. Use "at least as likely as not" verbatim.
  • No medical rationale — Asserting a connection without explaining why gives the VA grounds to assign the letter little weight.
  • No literature citations — A letter backed by published research is significantly harder to deny than a bare opinion.
  • Wrong provider type — The VA will scrutinize whether the provider's specialty matches the condition. A podiatrist writing a mental health nexus letter will be discounted.
  • Generic templates — The VA sees thousands of template letters. A letter that reads like it was filled out from a form carries far less weight than a specific, individualized opinion.
  • No review of records stated — If the letter doesn't say the provider reviewed your service treatment records and VA records, the VA can question whether the opinion is fully informed.

How to Get Your Provider to Sign It

This is where most veterans get stuck. Doctors are busy. Many have been told by VA leadership not to write nexus letters. Some are simply unfamiliar with the format.

The solution: do the work for them. Research the medical literature. Draft the letter in full. Bring it to your appointment with a request that they review it, modify as needed based on their clinical opinion, and sign it.

✓
What to say to your provider
"I've drafted a nexus letter for my VA disability claim. I've included my diagnosis, service history, and cited medical research supporting the connection. I'd like you to review it and sign it if you agree with the clinical opinion. This saves you the time of writing it from scratch — you just need to review and modify as needed."

Most NPs and PAs respond well to this approach. They want to help veterans — they just don't have hours to spend drafting letters from scratch. When you hand them a well-researched, properly formatted document, the barrier drops significantly.

What About Conditions Without a Clear In-Service Event?

Many veterans struggle with conditions that developed gradually or weren't documented during service. This is where the nexus letter becomes even more critical — and where the research matters most.

Under 38 CFR § 3.303(b), certain chronic conditions can be service-connected based on continuity of symptomatology — meaning if symptoms have been present since service, service connection can be established even without a specific documented incident. Your nexus letter should address this explicitly when applicable.

For conditions linked to military occupational hazards — noise exposure, chemical exposure, combat stress, sleep disruption — the medical literature supporting the connection is extensive. A well-researched letter citing that literature can bridge the gap where service records are incomplete.

Scoring Your Letter Before You Submit

One of the biggest mistakes veterans make is submitting a letter without any quality check. You have one shot at an initial claim. A weak letter filed now can set a poor effective date that costs you years of retroactive pay.

Before submitting, evaluate your letter against these five dimensions:

  1. CFR compliance — Does it cite the correct regulatory standard?
  2. Evidence strength — Is the in-service event clearly documented?
  3. Nexus quality — Is the causal chain explained, not just asserted?
  4. Literature support — Is the opinion backed by published research?
  5. Rater readiness — Is it formatted so a VA rater can find every required element in under 60 seconds?

A Note on Timing and Effective Dates

Your effective date — and therefore your retroactive pay — is tied to when you file. File now with a solid letter and establish your effective date, even if the claim needs development. Waiting until your letter is "perfect" costs you money. The VA backdates to the date of claim, not the date the evidence was submitted.
